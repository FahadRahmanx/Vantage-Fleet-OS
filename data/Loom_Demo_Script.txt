0:00  -  OPEN WITH THE PROBLEM, NOT THE TECH                       (40 seconds)
===============================================================================

SAY:
  Hi, I'm walking you through my response to the Vantage Fleet OS tender.
  Vantage moves freight, and today the work of getting a load out of the
  door is spread across separate steps: assign a driver and a truck,
  inspect the vehicle, dispatch it, then reconcile the paperwork and the
  driver's hours afterwards. They want one system that carries a load
  through all of that, where the workflow itself is configurable instead
  of hard-coded.

SAY:
  I read the whole requirement set, and honestly most of it is normal
  software. Three things are not. The configurable workflow engine, the
  hours-of-service ledger, and the eligibility rules that have to give the
  same answer on the server and in the driver's phone. Those three carry
  almost all of the risk. So instead of just writing a proposal, I built a
  working prototype of exactly those three, and I'll show you both.

  >> WHY THIS WORKS: you lead with their business problem, then name the
     hard parts. That is the 20% "understanding of requirements" criterion,
     earned in the first minute.


===============================================================================
0:40  -  THE PROPOSAL DOCUMENT                                     (90 seconds)
         Scores against EC-1 and EC-2, which are 55% of the marks
===============================================================================

DO:
  Switch to the PDF. Scroll steadily. Do NOT read it out.
  Land on each item for a few seconds.

SAY:
  This is the eight page proposal. I'll flag the parts that matter rather
  than read it to you.

SAY:
  Scope first. All fifty-seven requirements are mapped to one of seven
  delivery phases, so nothing is vague about what gets built when.

DO:
  Scroll to the technology stack table.

SAY:
  The stack. Their reference build is Django, and they allow alternatives
  if you justify it. I'm proposing TypeScript everywhere for one specific
  reason. Their own risk register says the eligibility rules could drift
  apart between the phone and the server, and says the best fix is a single
  source of truth. If the server is Python and the app is JavaScript, you
  cannot have one. With one language, the rules compile into a single
  package both sides import. So that risk stops being something you monitor
  and becomes something that cannot happen.

DO:
  Scroll to Figure 1, pause, then Figure 2.

SAY:
  Here's the architecture. Clients on top, one authorisation layer, then
  the shared rules package, then the four domain services, then the data.
  And this second diagram is the actual load workflow: the inspection in
  the middle decides which branch the load takes automatically.

DO:
  Scroll to the timeline bar and the phase table.

SAY:
  Thirty-four weeks across seven phases, inside the range they asked for.
  Every phase ends with something deployed and a specific thing I have to
  demonstrate to get it signed off. And the risky work is front-loaded,
  not saved for the end.

  >> DON'T read the tables aloud. Scrolling with commentary reads as
     confident. Reading aloud reads as padding.

3:35  -  THE INSPECTION DECIDES WHERE THE LOAD GOES                (60 seconds)
         Requirement FR-28
===============================================================================

DO:
  In "Change Status", select ASSIGNED, then click Advance.
  The DVIR panel appears.

SAY:
  I move it to Assigned, and the eligibility check runs again on the server
  before it's allowed through. Now a new panel appears: the driver
  inspection.

DO:
  Click "Submit DVIR". Show the defect list. Tick BRAKE FAILURE.

SAY:
  This is the pre-trip inspection a driver fills in. Each defect carries a
  severity. Cosmetic things are a pass, a worn wiper is a minor defect,
  brake failure takes the vehicle out of service. I'm going to report a
  brake failure.

DO:
  Submit. Land on the result screen.

SAY:
  And watch what happened. Nobody chose a next step. The system worked out
  the overall result from the defects, using the rule that the worst
  finding wins, and then routed the load automatically to Out of Service.
  That routing rule is configuration, not code. I'll show you where it's
  set in a minute.

  >> IF IT ERRORS: the load must be in ASSIGNED before you submit the
     inspection. Go back, advance it, then retry.

===============================================================================
KEEP THESE IN MIND WHILE RECORDING
===============================================================================

DO
  - Say requirement numbers out loud once or twice. It shows you worked
    from their document.
  - Pause for a beat after each result lands on screen.
  - Move the mouse deliberately, and slower than feels natural.
  - If you fumble, stop, breathe, say the sentence again. Cut it later.

AVOID
  - Reading tables or diagrams aloud word for word.
  - Apologising for anything that isn't built. State it plainly instead.
  - Calling it "just a POC". Call it a prototype of the highest-risk parts.
  - Running long. Under nine minutes keeps them watching to the end.

FOR A RETAKE
  Create a brand new load rather than reusing the one from the last take.
  Every take then starts from the same clean state, and the seeded demo
  loads stay untouched.
